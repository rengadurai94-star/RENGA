## FULL SCRIPT

### [0:00 — HOOK / 15 sec]
> Remember when summer vacation felt like it lasted forever? When one school year felt like a lifetime? Now you blink — and it's December again. You're not imagining it. Time really does feel like it's speeding up as you get older. And scientists figured out exactly why. Stick around — by the end of this video, I'll tell you the one trick that actually slows it back down.

### [0:15 — SETUP / 25 sec]
> Here's the wild part: physically, time isn't moving any faster. A second is still a second. A year is still 365 days. But your brain is processing all of it differently. Studies show that adults consistently report time feeling 20 to 30 percent faster than it did in their teens. And it gets worse every decade. There are actually three reasons this happens — and the third one is going to change how you think about your whole life.

### [0:40 — BEAT 1: PROPORTIONAL TIME / 25 sec]
> Reason number one — proportional time. When you're 5 years old, one year is 20 percent of your entire life. That's huge. By the time you're 50, one year is only 2 percent. So your brain weighs that year against everything else you've ever experienced — and it shrinks. Mathematicians call this the logarithmic theory of time perception. Basically, the older you get, the smaller each year feels compared to the ones before it.

### [1:05 — BEAT 2: NOVELTY / 30 sec]
> Reason two — novelty. Your brain records memories in much higher detail when something is new. As a kid, almost everything is new. First time at the beach. First day of school. First bike. Your brain captures all of it in vivid, sharp detail — which makes that year feel long and full. But once you're an adult? You drive the same route to work, eat the same lunch, see the same faces. Your brain stops recording. Whole weeks disappear because there's nothing new to log. This is called the "reminiscence bump." Almost everyone's most vivid memories are between ages 10 and 25 — exactly when life is most new.

### [1:35 — MID-ROLL RE-HOOK / 10 sec]
> If this is making you feel a little weird, hit that subscribe button — we explore stuff like this every week. Now, here comes the reason no one talks about.

### [1:45 — BEAT 3: DOPAMINE / 30 sec]
> Reason three — dopamine. Your brain's "internal clock" runs on dopamine. When you're young, dopamine is pumping. Every new experience releases a flood of it, and that flood literally stretches how time feels in the moment. As you age, dopamine production naturally drops. Less dopamine means your brain's clock ticks slower, which paradoxically makes external time feel faster. It's the same reason a boring meeting feels like 3 hours and a great date feels like 20 minutes — your dopamine is writing your timeline.

### [2:15 — BEAT 4: HOW TO SLOW IT DOWN / 35 sec]
> So here's the trick scientists agree on. To slow time back down, you have to give your brain new things to record. Not big things — small ones. Take a different route home. Eat lunch outside. Learn a chord. Talk to a stranger. Each novel moment forces your brain to start recording again — and that year, instead of vanishing, starts to feel full. People who travel, learn new skills, and break routines consistently report that their years feel longer. Not slower in a bad way — fuller. Richer. Like they got more out of the same 365 days.

### [2:50 — BEAT 5: THE KICKER / 25 sec]
> Here's the part that hits hardest. Researchers estimate that for most people, by age 25, you've already mentally experienced about half of your "felt" life. That doesn't mean half of your literal life — it means half of the time you'll remember. Because the next 50 years, if you stay in the same routine, will feel like a fast blur. But if you keep adding novelty? You can stretch the back half of your life much, much longer than the front.

### [3:15 — CLOSER / CTA / 15 sec]
> So no, you're not crazy. Time really is speeding up. But now you know exactly how to fight it. Try one new thing this week — and watch how much longer the week feels. If you liked this, you'll love our video on what really happens to your brain when you sleep. Subscribe so you don't miss it. Thanks for watching, and we'll see you on the bright side.
