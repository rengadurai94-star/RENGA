## KEY FACTS THIS SCRIPT IS BUILT ON

- **1946** — Earl Tupper, a DuPont-trained chemist, refines polyethylene slag into a durable, flexible plastic he calls "Poly-T," and molds it into lightweight, unbreakable kitchen containers.
- **The seal** — Tupper designs the container's airtight, "burping" seal by copying the inverted lip of a paint can lid — the single feature that made the product genuinely new.
- **The flop** — Sold on store shelves, Tupperware sits and fails. Shoppers don't understand how to "burp" the seal, and plastic still carries a cheap, low-trust reputation.
- **1948–51** — A saleswoman and single mother, working through a direct-sales distributor, discovers that Tupperware sells only when someone demonstrates it live, in a home, to a room of women. The in-home "Tupperware party" is born, and by 1951 Tupper pulls the product from every store shelf entirely, going 100% direct-sales.
- **The business model** — Tupperware parties give suburban women of the 1950s–60s a rare path to their own income and a sales title, at a time when many couldn't open a bank account or take out a loan without a husband's signature. At its peak, a Tupperware party starts somewhere in the world every 2.5 seconds.
- **The ouster** — The distribution-model architect who built the party system into a national force is pushed out of the company in 1958 without severance, credit publicly minimized soon after.
- **1958** — Tupper sells the company for roughly $16 million (a huge sum at the time) and largely disappears from public life.
- **Growth era** — Tupperware expands into over 100 countries, becomes a fixture of American suburban culture and, later, a major direct-sales force in Latin America and Asia.
- **1996** — Tupperware Corporation spins off from Premark International and lists on the NYSE.
- **Slow decline (2000s–2010s)** — Dollar stores, big-box retail, and rivals (Rubbermaid, Glad, Ziploc, generic imports) commoditize air-tight plastic storage. The door-to-door / home-party direct-sales model looks increasingly out of step with online retail. Recruiting new "consultants" gets harder every year.
- **COVID bump, then crash** — A brief 2020 pandemic sales bump (more home cooking, more leftovers) is followed by a steep drop-off as party gatherings resume other habits.
- **April 2023** — Tupperware Brands discloses "substantial doubt" about its ability to continue as a going concern. Stock craters.
- **Summer 2023** — Retail-trader "meme stock" attention briefly sends shares up hundreds of percent in days, echoing GameStop/AMC — pure momentum trading, disconnected from the underlying business.
- **September 2024** — Tupperware Brands Corporation files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in the U.S., listing debts that outweigh its assets.
- **Late 2024** — The brand and core assets are acquired out of bankruptcy by a group of the company's lenders, continuing on as a smaller, private operation rather than disappearing outright.
- **Legacy** — An original Tupperware bowl sits in the permanent collection of the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History as a landmark of industrial design and social history.
